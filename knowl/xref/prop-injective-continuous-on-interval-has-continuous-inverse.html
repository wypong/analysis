Proposition 4.4.14.

If \(f\) is injective and continuous on an interval \(I\text{,}\) then the inverse of \(f \colon I \to f(I)\) is also continuous.
🔗
🔗

Proof.
The statement is trivial if \(I\) is degenerated so let us assume \(I\) has at least two points. Since \(f\) is continuous and injective on an interval \(I\text{,}\) it is strictly monotone on \(I\text{,}\) say \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(I\) and hence so is its inverse \(g \colon f(I) \to I\text{.}\) So if \(g\) were discontinuous at some \(y_0 \in J:=f(I)\) then at least one of \(g(y_0^-)\) and \(g(y_0^+)\) must differ from \(g(y_0)\text{.}\) Without loss of generality, \(g(y_0^-)\text{,}\) which is the supremum of the set \(\{g(y) \colon y \in J, y \lt y_0\}\text{,}\) is strictly less than \(g(y_0)\text{.}\) From this, it is easy to see that \(g(y_0^-) \in I\) and so must \(x ':= (g(y_0^-)+g(y_0))/2\text{.}\) We then have \(y' \in J, y' \lt y_0\) with \(g(y') = x'\) contradicting the fact that \(g(y_0^-)\) is the supreme of \(g(J \cap (-\infty,y_0))\text{.}\)
